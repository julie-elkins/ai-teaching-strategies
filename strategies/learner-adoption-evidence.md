# Learner adoption evidence

## Strategy

Measure whether learners can use the intended practice, not only whether they
received or liked the material.

## Audience and context

Use this when deciding whether to retain, revise, expand, or retire a course,
lab, design guide, or video sequence.

## Teaching problem

Views, completions, attendance, or positive comments are easy to collect but do
not show whether the learner can make the target decision or recover from the
target failure.

## Facilitator move

Define the target behavior first. Pair delivery signals with bounded evidence:
an artifact, a repeatable task, a rubric result, a revision, a time-to-feedback
measure, or a safe transfer exercise. Keep model quality, learner performance,
and business or adoption outcomes as separate claims.

## Learner evidence

Use task-level outcomes, revision quality, explanation of limitations, and
repeated performance where the decision requires reliability. Report missing
evidence instead of converting it into a pass.

## Feedback and adaptation

Use the evidence to choose retain, clarify, revise, add practice, or retire.
Inspect subgroup and accessibility effects when data volume and consent permit.
Treat attention as part of the evidence budget. Before adding a feedback
request, use the working heuristic
`P(usable feedback) × expected change in the decision ÷ attention cost`.
Behavioral signals such as edits or abandonment can guide sampling, but they
are implicit feedback, not proof of learning or failure.

## Accessibility

Offer multiple ways to demonstrate the same capability and avoid requiring
public posting or personal data to participate.

## Responsible-use boundary

Collect the minimum data needed, state retention and access rules, and do not
infer learner identity, protected characteristics, or future job performance
from course interaction signals.

## Verification

Review the evidence definition with a human owner, test it on a small synthetic
or consented sample, and check that the result changes a real teaching decision.

## What this does and does not prove

It proves that adoption was defined as a behavior or decision rather than a
vanity metric. It does not prove broad population impact, causality, or
long-term retention from a small sample.
